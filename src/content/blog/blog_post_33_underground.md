# Weaponizing Rebellion — The Songbird Protocol

The Ashen Hand is Kharos-Vaul's resistance movement. It has safehouses, a command structure, sympathisers, and a reputation. It has been run by the empire for fifteen years.

That's the **Songbird Protocol**, and it's the piece of faction design I'm least comfortable with and most pleased by. The whole thing is operated by a retired agent of the Spymaster's service under the name Mother Ash. Every fifth safehouse carries a trigger-glyph that lays a Tünkurkan-drawn compulsion on the first non-human to enter, plus a recording sensor activated by a specific phrase — _"we need a place to hide"_ — which is, of course, the exact sentence anyone seeking refuge will say. The rebellion's command structure is compromised end to end. Anyone who joins is fed false intelligence and eventually walked into an ambush.

What makes it work as design rather than as a gotcha is that the Ashen Hand does real things. It has to. A fake resistance that never accomplishes anything recruits nobody, so the empire lets it win occasionally — small, real, survivable victories that keep the flow of genuine dissidents coming through the door where they can be catalogued. The people inside it are sincere. Their grievances are legitimate. Some of them have died for it. None of that is a lie; only the ownership is.

I want to be precise about why I built it. The Ashen Hand isn't there to punish the party for trusting people. It's there because it's what a competent authoritarian intelligence service would actually do, and because it makes the players' most reasonable instinct — find the local resistance, offer help — into the single most dangerous move available. That's a much sharper problem than a guard patrol.

The counterweight is the **Unbound Six**. Six people who have lost everything to the empire and are the only genuine uncaptured seditionists in the city. They've survived through paranoia and luck, and through one specific advantage: one of them invented a cantrip that senses scrying sensors, which is how they've stayed ahead of the Protocol for years. They know the Stone Ghost's tunnel and can guide a party under the Foundry Ring — and they will test the party ruthlessly first, because they've watched everyone else in their position get taken.

Six people. Not a network, not an army. That number is the point. In a city with a fabricated resistance of hundreds, the real one is small enough to fit in a room, and that's exactly why it still exists.

The vulnerability I gave the Protocol is a diary. Mother Ash keeps a private record of every person she has betrayed, hidden in her bedroom behind a lethal ward, containing the names of every imperial deep-cover agent in the city. It's the key to dismantling the whole operation. I gave her that for a reason that isn't mechanical: someone who has spent fifteen years destroying sincere people needs somewhere to put it, and a woman who keeps a list is a person, not a function. The thing that makes her human is the thing that can end her.

The Protocol also interacts with the city's other systems in a way I didn't plan and have kept. Kharos-Vaul's emergency anti-compulsion measure is indiscriminate — firing it cuts every active domination in the city, including the Songbird compulsions currently running. The empire's own counter-espionage weapon breaks its own counter-espionage program. Nobody designed that overlap. It emerged from two departments building tools without telling each other, which is the most realistic thing about the whole city.

One correction while I'm here, because I've seen the name misfiled in my own notes. The **Sealed Choir** is not an underground movement. It's the Sealed Choir of Hathra — clerics at Hikme's House of Silent Vows, sworn to not hear, see, or speak, serving in rotating pairs so every binding rite is witnessed by four of them. They're entirely above board, deliberately expensive to maintain, pantheon-neutral, and they never testify about what passes through their hands. They're a religious order built for confidentiality, which is a different thing from a conspiracy, and I'd conflated the two purely because both involve people who don't talk.

The broader lesson from building the Songbird Protocol is one I've applied elsewhere since: a faction is more interesting when you know who owns it than when you know what it wants. The Ashen Hand's stated goals are perfectly clear and completely irrelevant. What matters is the answer to a question nobody in the fiction thinks to ask.
