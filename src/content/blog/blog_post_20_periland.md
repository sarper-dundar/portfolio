# Periland — The Fey Plane

D&D's Feywild is a place of beauty with a cruel streak. Periland isn't cruel. It's the plane of chaotic creation, and almost everything unpleasant about it is a side effect of that rather than malice.

The plane runs on unrestrained creation energy. Forests sprout and vanish, rivers change course, mountains rise and fall inside a moment, and time itself runs erratically. The air carries so much creative potential that it's nearly impossible for anything inside it to stay calm — its inhabitants live in more or less permanent euphoria, and that euphoria is the engine of everything they do.

So the fairies of Periland aren't tricksters by nature. They're overstimulated. They leave their plane out of curiosity and mischief rather than conquest, and they pull pranks because the energy running through them has to go somewhere. What makes them worth having around is the side effect: they carry the energy of life with them, and plants grow faster and more abundantly in their wake without any of them intending it. They're revered simultaneously as nuisances and as creators of life, and both reputations are accurate.

Above them are the **Fey Lords and Ladies**, embodying the strongest aspects of creation. Their whims shift like weather and their influence over the plane is total — a gesture makes a forest, footsteps leave flowers. They're not evil. They're unpredictable, which in a being that powerful is functionally worse.

The owner is **Lirael, the Whim of Creation**, a radiant shifting figure of light and colour, always in motion — and a Plane Owner in Kazankor doesn't govern their plane, they _are_ it, expressed as a being. Periland's moods aren't obedience to Lirael. They're hers, happening at landscape scale. She treats creation as an endless game and delights in her ability to remake life. She also gets bored easily, abandons projects without thinking about them again, and sends her subjects to meddle with mortals for entertainment. When she's happy, Periland flourishes. When she's upset or weakened, creation there becomes erratic — bursts of growth with nothing controlling them, which is dangerous in ways that have nothing to do with anyone wanting to hurt you.

The part that matters at the table is how bargains work, because I gave Periland four explicit laws and they turn negotiation into a solvable puzzle instead of a guaranteed betrayal.

**The letter of the agreement.** Fey follow the exact wording of a bargain and have no interest whatsoever in its spirit. Not because they're looking for loopholes to exploit — because the words _are_ the agreement to them.

**Equivalent exchange.** What's given must equal what's received. This one is genuinely fair, with the catch that value gets judged by fey logic, which can differ wildly from a mortal's. A memory of a specific afternoon might be worth more to them than a kingdom.

**The law of hospitality.** Manners matter enormously. Rudeness or ingratitude can invalidate a pact outright or give real offence, which means a player can lose a negotiation by being brusque while getting every term right.

**Time is fluid.** Duration is difficult to gauge in a plane where time runs erratically, so any agreement with a term measured in time is a gamble on what that time means.

Those four rules mean a careful player can win. They can draft precisely, offer something the fey will overvalue, be gracious, and avoid time-denominated terms. That's a real skill test rather than a DM ambush, and I much prefer running it that way. When a fey bargain goes badly in my game it's because somebody was sloppy, and they can usually see exactly where.

The contract types run from a Trickster's Bargain — simple terms with clever gaps, where you end up owing more than you expected — through favour-for-favour arrangements, blood oaths, exchanging years of your lifespan for immediate mastery, and at the far end a soul pact. Standard escalation, but the four laws apply the whole way up, so even the soul pact is negotiable if you're careful enough about wording.

What Periland charges is what it has too much of: joy, beauty, positive memory, personal charm, the sense of wonder. A long-term Periland practitioner ends up capable of remarkable creation and progressively less able to enjoy anything, which is the most quietly sad cost in the system and the one I find myself using most.

The tradeoff with running the plane this way is that Periland is much less frightening than a traditional Feywild, and some of that is a loss. There's a specific horror in fey who genuinely want to hurt you, and I've given that up. What I got in exchange is a plane where the danger comes from carelessness and from being near something enormously powerful that isn't paying attention — which fits a world where the real threat is a system rather than a villain.
