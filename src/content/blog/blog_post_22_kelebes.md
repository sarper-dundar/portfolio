# Kelebes — The Plane of Balance

Kelebes is the ninth plane and the one I nearly forgot to write about, which is fitting, because being overlooked is more or less its function.

The other eight planes do something. Periland creates, Kazırgan ends, Tözyar holds the laws, Tünmorin recycles the dead. Kelebes prevents. It's a thin ethereal membrane stretched between the True Source and its Opposite, keeping two forces apart whose meeting would unravel the cosmos. Neither creation nor destruction has any purchase there. Nothing grows and nothing dies. Time barely moves, if it moves at all.

It also touches every other plane that draws on either force, which makes it the only place in my cosmology that is genuinely everywhere. Most beings will never perceive it. It sits beneath notice by design, silently absorbing the overflow from both sides so the two never mix enough to matter.

Its inhabitants are the **Aksor**, and they're the most unsettling thing I've written. They're beings of pure balance with no free will and no desires — closer to sentient mechanisms than to living creatures. They don't patrol out of duty because duty implies a choice. They wait, inert, until something triggers them: a breach in the barrier, or a direct instruction. Then they act with cold efficiency, seal the gap, and stop again. They don't care about morality or about anything happening on the planes they're protecting. Small leaks don't interest them. Only large disturbances wake them up.

I find them frightening in a way the fallen angels of Tamaa are not, because you cannot negotiate with something that wants nothing and cannot be provoked by anything short of the specific condition it's built to detect.

The owner is **Ertene, the Eternal Watcher**, and this is where the plane connects to everything else in the setting — and where the usual rule bends. Every other Plane Owner simply _is_ their plane expressed as a being. Ertene is one of the **Üçler**, the Three, and his ownership of Kelebes is a different arrangement: the barrier exists because he is holding it, not because it is him. He appears as a colossal figure with the wings of the great white goose he once was, his form shifting between light and shadow without ever settling into either. He's detached and emotionless. He has no personal desires. He doesn't interfere with gods or mortals unless the balance itself is threatened, and he'll tolerate small spills indefinitely as long as the scales hold.

The reason that matters: this is the same Ertene whose sleep produced the dream-dimension, and the same Ertene whose eternal flight over the primordial water preceded the existence of anything else. He is simultaneously the oldest thing in my cosmology, the guardian of the barrier, and — asleep — the origin of an entire plane of dreams. I did not plan that overlap. It emerged from writing the pieces separately and noticing they were the same figure, and I've kept it because it explains something useful: the being holding creation apart from its opposite is not fully present. He is doing the job in his sleep.

What Kelebes does for the rest of the setting is act as a limiter. Tamaa's inhabitants occasionally manage to touch the Opposite while wielding the True Source, and the result verges on omnipotent and threatens reality outright. The reason that's _rare_ rather than routine is Kelebes. The barrier is why the forbidden thing stays forbidden in practice and not just in law, and if Ertene is ever weakened or distracted enough for the veil to thin, the constraint on the single most dangerous act in my cosmology gets looser.

That's a doomsday clock I can advance without introducing anything new. Nobody needs to invade Kelebes. Something only has to tire out the sleeping goose.

There's no Outsource connection to this plane and there shouldn't be. Kelebes has nothing to lend — no domain, no power, no beings with preferences to bargain with. A practitioner reaching for it would find a wall and some machines. That's the correct answer for a plane whose entire nature is separation, and it means Kelebes stays a piece of cosmology rather than a resource.

If it has a weakness as a design, it's that a plane defined by absence is hard to put in front of players. My honest expectation is that most tables will only ever encounter Kelebes as an explanation — the reason a catastrophe didn't happen, offered after the fact by someone who knows more than they should. That's a smaller role than the other eight get. For the thing holding the universe together, invisibility seems about right.
