# Uştmay — The Celestial Plane

Uştmay is the closest thing Kazankor has to a heaven, and I built it so that nobody would want to argue with it and everybody would end up doing exactly that.

It's the plane of lawful creation — the idea that making things must be structured and intentional rather than left to chance. Everything in it operates with precision. Time flows predictably. Nothing exists without a reason tied to the good of the universe. Its inhabitants, the **Uştmayans**, were created by gods specifically to protect creation and maintain balance, and they are immensely powerful and utterly dutiful.

They are also, in my own notes, rigid and short-sighted. That's the whole design.

Uştmayans are highly intelligent and pair that intelligence with an unreasonable focus on whatever mission they were given. Once assigned, they execute with total devotion and disregard any harm arising along the way. Their tunnel vision blinds them to solutions that would be obvious to anyone willing to compromise, and their inability to compromise means they sometimes cause exactly the destruction they were sent to prevent. They are good in the sense of being aligned with creation and balance. They are not kind, and they are not flexible.

The hierarchy is the detail I like most. Uştmayans serve different gods, including gods hostile to one another, and they coexist on the plane without fighting. Their shared purpose outranks their patrons' rivalries. Rank within Uştmay isn't determined by power or by whose god is winning — it's determined by degree of servitude. The more completely you have given yourself to your mission, the higher you stand.

So Uştmay is a place where enemies work in the same building, peaceably, because none of them consider their own god's agenda more important than the structure they all maintain. That's a genuinely alien social arrangement and it makes celestial politics interesting instead of a colour-coded army.

The owner is **Ilimar, the Guardian's Command** — regal, clad in armour of pure light, ruling with an unbending hand. Though "ruling" isn't quite right: a Plane Owner is their plane expressed as a being, so Uştmay's rigidity isn't a policy Ilimar enforces. It's what he is. He's disciplined and uncompromising and views even minor disorder as a threat. He clashes constantly with beings who hold more flexible views, which includes most gods and effectively all mortals. If Ilimar falters, the plane fractures: its inhabitants become confused, inefficient, and prone to mistakes, which is a much more frightening failure state than the plane turning evil.

What Uştmay charges practitioners is the thing it values. Its sacrifices are personal freedom, personal choice, individuality and creativity. You pay for lawful creation with the parts of yourself that are unlawful and uncreated — the improvisational parts. That's a cost most players don't feel until they're several sessions in and notice their character has stopped surprising them.

The mechanics run on the same principle, and they're the only ones in the Outsource system that reward restraint. **Service Points** accumulate when a practitioner casts precisely, without improvisation or last-minute adjustment, and when they avoid chaotic action generally — deception, impulsiveness. Enough points and defensive spells reach further, concentration steadies, and they gain resistance to charm, fear and confusion. Act chaotically and the points bleed away and the bonuses reset.

On top of that, **Binding Rituals** let a practitioner buy power by accepting a restriction in advance. Take a vow of silence for the encounter and one spell gets stronger. Confine yourself to a ten-foot radius and everything lasts half again as long. Swear off offensive action entirely and your protective spell doubles. Break the binding mid-encounter and you lose concentration and your access to Uştmay for a day.

I like this because it makes the celestial plane mechanically _narrow_ rather than mechanically strong. A good Uştmay practitioner is someone who plans, commits, and doesn't deviate. In a party that improvises, they're the person who declared what they were doing three rounds ago and is still doing it.

The tradeoff is that Uştmay is the hardest plane to play alongside other people. Its bonuses punish the exact behaviour that makes a party fun — the sudden idea, the change of plan, the lie told to a guard. I've had one player run it well and they did it by treating the restriction as the character rather than as a tax. That's the only way it works, and it isn't for everyone.
