# The Alchemist Class

True Source is the thing the gods are harvesting, and no mortal can channel it directly. The Alchemist is my answer to the obvious follow-up question: what happens to someone who tries anyway?

The class starts from one rule. A formula is not a potion. A potion is consumed and passes through. A formula does not pass through — it finds something in the body, some threshold of biology and True Source, and rewrites it permanently. Alchemists are insistent on this distinction and will correct you if you get it wrong. They are not enhanced. They are changed.

That single rule generated the whole class, because it forces every design question to be about direction rather than power. An Alchemist doesn't ask what they can do this turn. They ask what they are becoming. So instead of a spell list, the class has **Paths** — fifty-one of them, which is more than any campaign will ever see and I'm at peace with that.

A Path is a direction of transformation, and the choice is permanent — paths cannot be mixed, reordered, paused, or resumed. You pick once and that is what you are becoming. Hollow Alchemists remove things: sentiment, biological need, hesitation, warmth. What's left after years is efficient and difficult to stop, and by the end of the path it stops casting a shadow in natural light. Bloom Alchemists become gardens — bread lasts longer near them, wounds close faster, and their understanding of growth eventually includes the kind that chokes a field into an impassable wall in days. Forge Alchemists become architecture, load-bearing and anchored, teaching anyone who tries to move them the difference between a body and a building. Burning Word Alchemists make speech land somewhere the body can't ignore, and by the end, ten words can make something briefly and literally true.

The fifty-one split into five groups, and mostly by how findable they are rather than how strong. Fifteen are general — documented, discussed, and a motivated seeker can in principle find a teacher. Fifteen more are obscure: suppressed, rumored, or absent from public record. Five are legendary proper. Six are the evil legendary paths. And eleven are failed or incomplete — badly built, unfinished, or designed to lead whoever walks them toward destruction. I wrote those last eleven down specifically because a player may encounter one, and recognition is more useful than ignorance.

Progression runs through ten tiers of formula, unlocked slowly — Tier I at level 2, Tier X at level 28. Higher tiers aren't just bigger numbers; they're gated by ingredients that don't sit in shops. Tier V and up need quests, faction relationships, or acquisitions with a moral cost attached. An Alchemist can only use their own path's formulas, too. Drinking someone else's gets you three Corruption and no benefit.

Which brings me to the part I'm most pleased with. Every formula carries a **Digestion Condition** — a behavioral requirement the Alchemist has to fulfil between drinking it and their next long rest, which in Kazankor is a week. Meet it and the change settles cleanly. Fail to meet it and you take two Corruption. The conditions are roleplayed, not rolled; the DM judges whether the spirit of the thing was genuinely met, and partial effort earns partial credit.

The Corruption Track runs 0 to 10 and the body does not politely wait for the far end. At 1–2 it's cosmetic — skin discoloration, an odd shimmer in the eye, a reagent smell soap won't shift. At 3–4 the transformation is visible enough to unsettle people who know what they're looking at. At 5–6 you roll on the minor mutation table at the start of every long rest, permanently. At 7–8 it's the major table, and the fraying starts reaching how you perceive and process. At 9 you're at the Dissolution Edge, making a save to avoid tipping over, and the edge always leaves a mark whichever way you leave it. At 10 you make one Constitution save against Dissolution. Succeed and you reset to 7, permanently changed. Fail and the character is gone — the body continues as an NPC driven entirely by the path's deepest instincts.

The design note I wrote for myself on the mutation tables is the one I'd defend hardest: mutations are not punishments. They are the path speaking ahead of schedule. A player who rolls a mutation at Corruption 5 should feel the world noticing before they were ready to be noticed.

Tier XI is the last thing, and it isn't a mortal tier. Every tier before it involved True Source rewriting the body toward something. At Tier XI the relationship inverts — the body stops being shaped by True Source and becomes something that shapes it. The Alchemist isn't channeling a field anymore; they are a True Source event, leaving field impressions behind them as they walk.

That's also where the class's real privilege sits, and it isn't the one people assume. Ascending to Illumined Monarch takes level 30 and a **god seed** — neither requirement mentions a class, so anyone who reaches thirty and finds a seed can do it. What an Alchemist can do is _make_ one. Their path produces the object everyone else has to hunt for, which makes theirs the only guaranteed route to godhood rather than the only permitted one. That keeps the setting's rule intact all the way to the top: nothing is locked by what you are, only by what you can get hold of.

The honest costs of building it this way are two. Fifty-one paths is a lot of writing for content most tables will never open. And the whole cost structure rests on Digestion Conditions being enforced — a DM who waves them through as flavor text has removed the class's entire spine and left a very durable spellcaster behind. Practitioners in-world say the formulas are the easy part, and becoming someone who can genuinely meet the conditions is the work. That's true of running the class, too.
