# Kazırgan — The Infernal Plane

The most useful decision I made about Kazırgan is that its inhabitants don't enjoy their work.

Kazırgan is the plane of lawful destruction, the counterweight to Periland's unrestrained creation. Its destruction is precise, measured and deliberate — buildings crumble slowly, stone erodes over time, even death happens in an orderly fashion. There's no chaos in it. Destruction here is a form of order, on the reasoning that reducing things to their simplest forms is what makes room for new creation elsewhere.

Its creatures are cunning and cold and take fiendish or shadowy forms, and they measure the gain and loss of every act. Efficiency and control are their highest values. They do leave the plane to destroy things, but out of duty rather than appetite, and the more they destroy the more powerful they become — always deliberately, always with a purpose behind each strike. Above them sit the **Kazır Lords**, master strategists who plan every act of destruction for maximum efficiency and genuinely consider themselves the balancers of the cosmos, keeping creation from spiralling out of control.

The owner is **Ragûl, the Silent End** — and as with every Plane Owner in Kazankor, he doesn't preside over Kazırgan, he _is_ Kazırgan expressed as a being. Dark, ethereal, imposing, moving with deliberate sharpness. He's stoic and emotionless, takes no pleasure in destruction, and calculates several steps ahead of everyone. His only interest is that nothing grows unchecked and nothing lasts forever. When Ragûl is focused, Kazırgan runs smoothly and destruction stays measured. When he's weakened, it becomes erratic — and erratic destruction in the lawful plane is worse than more of it, because the thing that made it survivable was its predictability.

This is why the infernal contract system is the clearest in the setting, and it's a direct consequence of the personalities. Beings who calculate everything write contracts you can read. There's a published hierarchy — lesser demons and imps at the bottom, mid-tier demons above them, high devils and archfiends above those, infernal lords at the top — and each rank has a stated price and a stated offering. Minor offerings and petty acts buy small curses and tricks. Significant sacrifices and soul fragments buy real destructive magic and control over minions. Long-term service and massive sacrifice buys soul manipulation and transformation. Eternal servitude and genuinely heinous acts buy world-altering effects.

The consequences are published too, and they scale the same way. A deal with a lesser demon carries limited risk and breaks easily. Mid-tier deals are binding and dangerous to break. High devil contracts are complex with severe penalties written into them. At the top, breaking the arrangement means your power stripped without warning, a betrayer's curse, or being dragged bodily into the plane.

I want to be precise about why I find this the most _honest_ of my planes. Kazırgan will tell you the price. It will hold to the terms. It will not surprise you with a technicality, because technicalities are inefficient and the beings here despise waste. What it will do is offer you an escalating series of accurate bargains, each of which you evaluate correctly and accept anyway. Nobody who ends up owned by an archdevil in my setting was tricked. They read the contract and signed it because they wanted the thing on the other side.

That's a different kind of horror from the fey version and I think it's the better one. Periland punishes carelessness. Kazırgan punishes wanting something too much.

Loyalty pays, which is the part that makes it seductive over a long campaign. Reliable service earns infernal favour — greater potency, less backlash — then access to hidden rituals and ancient texts, then transformation into a devil outright with the immortality attached, then standing as an archdevil's champion operating across the planes. Every step is a genuine promotion, and every step is offered to someone who has already demonstrated they'll take it.

What Kazırgan charges is blood and life force and vitality first, and then moral boundary, innocence, and your sense of right and wrong. The physical currency is what a new practitioner notices. The second column is what they're actually spending.

The tradeoff of building the infernal plane this way is that I lost the classical devil — the honeyed liar who twists your words. My devils are chartered accountants with terrifying resources, and some of the theatrical fun of infernal negotiation is gone. What I got instead is a plane where the players understand the deal perfectly and the tension comes entirely from watching a character decide to take it anyway.
