# Tamga — The Inscription Economy

Tamga is the system I expect most players to end up using, because it's the only one you can learn by deciding to. No pact, no awakening, no formula that rewrites your body. You find a teacher, you study, you get good at drawing. Intelligence is the stat. That's the whole gate.

What makes it interesting to design is that the power of a Tamga doesn't come from the person drawing it. It comes from the drawing. Accuracy, complexity, and scale determine what happens, which means a careful amateur with time and a good surface can produce something a rushed expert can't. I wanted at least one system where preparation beats talent, and this is it.

Mechanically it's a budget. Every effect is assembled from **Effect Points**, and your EP cap is just your Tamga level times five — level 1 gives you 5 EP, level 10 gives you 50. Then you buy the effect you actually want. Twenty feet of range costs 1. An extra d6 of damage costs 2. Five feet of radius costs 2. A shield worth 5 temporary HP costs 3. A major status effect for one turn costs 5. You can also sell power back for budget — drop a d6 of damage and reclaim 2 EP — which is where the system starts feeling less like casting and more like engineering.

The real cost isn't EP, though. It's time. A small sigil takes one action, a medium one takes one or two, and a large one takes three or four. You are vulnerable the entire time you're drawing, and if you take damage you're rolling an Intelligence check to hold the inscription together. You can rush it with a Sleight of Hand check against DC 15 for something basic or 20-plus for something complex, and every immediate retry raises the DC by 2. A critical success doubles your effect metrics. A bad failure backfires.

That drawing window is doing most of the design work. It means Tamga is genuinely excellent when the practitioner chose the ground and genuinely awkward when the fight came to them. A Tamga inscriber who gets thirty seconds of warning is one of the most dangerous people in a room. One who gets ambushed is holding a piece of chalk.

Surface matters as much as size. A Tamga is strongest when the effect acts on the surface it's drawn on — roughly half again as potent — and weaker when it reaches off the surface into open space. Stone and metal hold protective and durability work well. Parchment travels. Air and water are fast and unstable. Inks scale the same way, from basic training ink up through elemental sources to the rare materials that extend duration or stabilize something that would otherwise wobble.

There's also a preparation mode that I like more the longer the campaign runs. Because a finished sigil just needs a moment of will to activate, an inscriber can lay work down in advance — traps armed ahead of time, defensive sigils sitting ready to fire as a reaction, scrolls bound to their own will rather than usable by anyone who picks them up. Tamga inscribers don't carry spells. They carry infrastructure.

The part that ties Tamga back to the setting is the material. Inscription only works permanently because of **Tamutash** — the indestructible dust that forms when True Source is used, accumulating over millennia into ore. Tamutash is what lets a pattern hold power without deteriorating, and it is the thing the gods engineer wars to harvest. So the most learnable, most civic, most democratic magic in the world runs on the world's central commodity. Tamga is why Tamutash has a price, and Tamutash's price is why the harvest exists.

That has consequences I didn't fully plan for and have come to enjoy. Institutions form around inscription because inscription is infrastructure. Hikme has a Tamga Tower for the discipline and a separate Wards Tower whose entire mandate is engineering — intake, triage, disassembly, and rehousing of magical devices into things a city can safely use, a wand's heat core becoming a cook-well. Their motto is "form follows safety." Kharos-Vaul's defenses are layered inscription work. None of that came from a rules decision. It came from having a magic system that behaves like a trade.

The tradeoff is bookkeeping again, and a specific kind of it. EP menus invite optimization, and a player who enjoys spreadsheets will find the efficient combinations quickly. I've decided I don't mind. The counterweight is that the efficient build still has to stand still and draw, in front of people who would prefer it didn't.

One honest note on balance. Tamga has real defensive use — shields on the EP menu, pre-inscribed barriers that fire as a reaction, and at civic scale the ward work that holds cities together. But in a practitioner's hands it skews offensive, and I've stopped pretending otherwise. The wards are mostly infrastructure built by institutions over years, not something an adventuring inscriber throws up mid-fight. Those are two different crafts that happen to share an alphabet.
