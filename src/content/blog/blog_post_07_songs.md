# Songs of Creation

Songs of Creation are not music, and the distinction matters for how the system plays. A Song is a sequence the universe recognizes — a pattern woven into existence at its founding and since misplaced rather than lost. The practitioner isn't casting anything. They're reminding the world of something it already knows.

That framing gave me the cost structure for free. If reality is being moved _through_ a person, the person is the instrument, and instruments wear. The voice, the breath, the hands are not metaphors here. Something passes through and leaves marks.

The mechanical shape is a Song that gets harder every turn you hold it. Starting one costs an action and requires you to be able to make sound and move freely. From then on, at the start of each of your turns, you apply that turn's effect and then make two saves against the Song's current DC — Constitution and Wisdom. Then the DC goes up by one. Fail the Constitution save and you take a point of **Strain**. Fail the Wisdom save and you take a point of **Fracture**. Fail both in the same turn and something visible happens: the voice cracks, a string snaps, blood from the nose or ears.

Starting DCs run from 8 for simple folk Songs up to 20 for a complete divine one, and since the DC climbs by one per turn, a Song opened at 17 is at 22 by turn six. You can drop a Song any time on your turn at no cost. The Strain and Fracture stay.

Two tracks rather than one was the important call. Strain is physical and it takes your body apart from the bottom up — checks first, then saves, then speed, then attacks made at disadvantage, then a save every turn to avoid falling prone, and at ten your body simply won't continue. Fracture is mental and it takes something different: focus, then perception, then an echo of the Song audible for an hour after it ends, then sensory intrusions, and at eight you cannot hold a sustained pattern at all — no concentration, no Songs. They aren't interchangeable and they don't cover for each other. A singer can be physically fine and mentally unable to begin, or clear-headed and unable to stand.

Then every Song has a **Fault** on top of that, specific to itself. Faults aren't failure results — you pay them whether the Song goes well or badly. They're the price the universe extracts for the particular thing being asked. Songs that protect take something from the singer's own safety. Songs that harm leave residue in the singer's perception. Writing them per Song rather than as a general rule is more work, but it means the answer to "what does this Song cost" is never "the usual."

There's one clean, deliberately unfair interaction: thunder damage ends a Song immediately, no save. The resonance disrupts the pattern irreversibly. Anyone who knows a Song practitioner is in the room and has access to a thunderclap has a hard counter, and I like that this exists. Every system in Kazankor should have something that switches it off.

The other structural piece is completeness. Songs are almost never whole. They were given in fragments by gods with reasons for withholding the rest, learned from sources that never had the full version, or worn down over centuries of transmission. What you know determines how many turns of the sequence actually exist for you — a Fragment gets you one to three turns, a Partial four to six, Extended seven to nine, Complete ten or more. A practitioner holding a Fragment can't reach turn four no matter how long they hold on. The sequence just ends.

That turns Song advancement into a treasure structure instead of a level structure. You don't get better at a Song. You find more of it. Complete Songs sit in ancient texts, in the memory of beings who were present at the making, or in places where the boundary between creation and now is thin.

And you cannot learn a Song from a score. This is the rule I get asked about most. The reason is that what transmits isn't the notes — it's the felt understanding of what the pattern is doing, and that only passes person to person. Mechanically it means learning one Song takes three to six weeks with a practitioner who already knows it, every Song needs its own teacher, and some traditions teach freely while others want something that isn't money. A player who wants to be good at Songs is committing to a campaign of finding people.

The tradeoff is that Songs are slow to acquire and easy to shut down, which makes them a poor primary for a player who wants reliability. What they offer instead is the highest ceiling of any system a starting character can actually reach, paid for in escalating risk that the player chooses to keep taking, one turn at a time. Every turn a Song stays up is a decision. That's the whole design.
