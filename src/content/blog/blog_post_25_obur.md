# The Obur — Blood and Its Price

The Obur are the one race in Kazankor where I wrote the document in two halves: what they believe about themselves, and what is actually true. The players get the first half. They earn the second one.

What the Obur believe is that their ancestors were marked by something divine and their veins now carry the ability to bend blood — their own, their enemies', blood already spilled on the ground. They call it **Sanguine Power**. They aren't vampires and they'll correct you if you say so: they feed because the power in their veins consumes them from the inside without it, not because they're undead. They live several centuries, sometimes a millennium, among other races, usually hidden, often suspected. Most people who work out what they are decide not to mention it.

They don't agree on where they came from, and I gave them three regional traditions rather than one myth. The western clans tell of the First Feast — starving ancestors drinking from the wound of a bleeding, unnamed god, healed by blood they would afterwards always need. The eastern clans tell of the Crimson Pact, a bargain trading their original mortality for what runs in them now, meant to last a generation and still running after half a million years. The northern clans tell the strangest one: a moon that isn't Kazankor's, bleeding, and ancestors who drank from it.

Most Obur dismiss the Bleeding Moon as poetic nonsense, on the grounds that moons don't bleed and no foreign moon has ever been visible in the sky. Most Obur are wrong. It's the only tradition that names the source, and it's the closest to the truth.

The truth is that they are a **Wilder lineage of True Source channelers**. What they call blood magic is direct channeling of the field, expressed through a Wilder Engel that uses blood as its conduit — and the Engel is shared across the entire lineage rather than being individual. Every Obur, born or made, has the same block: they cannot embrace the field directly the way a Tözen does. Their channeling always passes through blood. Without that medium, nothing happens at all.

I like this because it means the Obur's own vocabulary is accurate as far as it goes. Sanguine Power, the Vein, the blood-art — internally consistent, correct in every observable respect, and disconnected from the framework that would explain it, because nobody in current Kazankor has that framework. They've been describing their power correctly and understanding it wrongly for half a million years. A Plane Owner would see what they are instantly.

The Engel also explains the feeding, which was the piece I was most pleased to land. Over hundreds of thousands of years of constant use, their physiology fused its biological needs to its channeling needs — the same medium for both. So a starving Obur is losing two things at once: their nutrition and their conduit. The two haven't been separable for a very long time, which is why deprivation hits them so fast and so hard.

Mechanically their Sanguine Power is a Cürek pool with different rules. It doesn't refill on rest; it refills by feeding. The pools run smaller than a Tözen's at equivalent level, because a single permitted conduit is a real restriction. In exchange they can refill mid-combat by drawing from open wounds, which no Tözen can do. Capability sorts into five tiers, from minor wound-shaping up to apex work, and none of it is learned as spellcraft — an Obur child manifests the basic forms instinctively, the way they learn to walk. An old Obur is dangerous not because they have more power but because they've had centuries to work out what to do with what they have.

The other cost is what deprivation does to control. Held too long without blood, or pushed past their emotional limits, the power burns through restraint and expresses itself uncontrolled — violent, usually destructive, and not directable by the Obur it's coming out of. That's the thing an Obur character actually manages at the table. Not a hunger meter. The knowledge that going hungry makes them a hazard to the people standing near them.

And behind all of it sits the history none of them have. Around half a million years ago an unidentified god set a trap, caught a large number of Obur from elsewhere in the cosmos, and brought them here as a forced mercenary force. What that purpose was, whether it was achieved, and what became of the god are all unknown. They were bound here against their will, lost contact with wherever they came from, and forgot. The originals died; their grandchildren grew up knowing only Kazankor. By the time anyone was writing history down, they were from here.

The part that reframes it is that this was not unusual. The gods stock Kazankor — they take species and whole races from other worlds and put them here to keep the place populated, because a farm with too few people on it produces too little. The Obur weren't singled out by a villain. They were inventory, moved by a god doing routine work, and the reason their capture is documented at all is that they're the case I've written down rather than the only one.

They do remember one piece of it, under a name of their own: the **First Tapping**, the desperate covenant where their ancestors drank from the well to save themselves. Every clan tradition is a distorted retelling of that. What none of them know is that it wasn't a local event — it was the collision between a people from outside the system and the rawest True Source access point on the planet, and what came out of it is something the Luminous Ones had never seen before and still don't fully understand.

The trap may still be in place. No Obur currently knows to test it.
