# Octarion and Other Rare Races

Not every population in a setting needs to be campaign-relevant. Some exist because the world is more convincing with them in it, and occasionally one turns out to be useful in a way I didn't plan. Two of mine have earned their space.

The **Octarion** are nomadic, octopus-like, and built around a single good idea: their ink is their writing system.

They're large and muscular with eight segmented limbs, skin that shifts colour and texture for camouflage, and the ability to regrow lost tentacles — slowly, painfully, over weeks, draining enough that they only rely on it in emergencies. They can produce ink that hardens on contact into improvised clubs, shields, or spikes, brittle and temporary but enough for a moment that matters. Learning to do that is their coming-of-age.

What makes them worth writing is **Inkcasting**. They communicate partly through ink patterns — colours and shapes carrying meaning, dark swirls for danger, luminescent streaks for joy — painted into water or onto cave walls, and readable only by other Octarion. They layer it over a vocal language of clicks and whistles and hums, so a full Octarion conversation carries information and emotion on two channels at once. And older Octarion wear their history: intricate ink markings across their skin marking milestones and clan heritage, so an elder is a legible document of their own life.

Their society is three roles and a set of ancestral obligations. **Pathfinders** lead, chosen for reading currents and terrain rather than for strength, and gather periodically in a Council of the Depths to trade information about changing conditions and settle disputes between clans. **Firesmiths** forge at volcanic vents, working stone and coral into weapons and tools. **Speakers** hold the clan's history and tell it, painting scenes in ink on rock. They return to sacred volcanic sites for rites, they believe their ancestors' spirits — the Elder Tides — live in the rifts and deep caves, and they mate during the Molt of the Deep because they hold that volcanic energy strengthens the young.

They're vulnerable to cold and to sunlight, dry out on land within days, and lose their camouflage under prolonged bright light. So they're capable, respected, and structurally unable to project power onto the surface, which is exactly what I want from a race that should enrich the map without reshaping the plot.

The **Automaton Harvesters** are the other one, and they're a different kind of useful.

They're ancient machines abandoned by a civilization that no longer exists, built as multi-purpose constructs to assist a society whose requirements are lost. They live in the depths of the Forgotten Arteries now, sitting on enormous mineral reserves they guard fiercely, and they are permanently short of the one thing they actually need. They run on True Source, which means Tamutash for energy stabilization and Agyntash for constructs resistant to magical interference — so the machines are locked into the same scarcity as everyone else in Kazankor, competing for the substance the gods are farming.

That constraint produces their whole behaviour. Losing a single unit is expensive because replacing it costs Tamutash they can't easily get, so they're cautious to the point of paralysis about venturing upward. They experiment with expendable drones. They consider alliances with surface factions. A machine civilization that is careful because its people are costly is a much better neighbour than one that swarms.

Their goal is to restore their **Central AI**, the system that once organised them, now fragmented and barely functional with most of its data corrupted. Doing it needs rare resources, knowledge, and probably the help of intelligent mortals — which is as clean a quest hook as I've ever written, because the party is being recruited into something sympathetic by people who cannot lie about what they want.

The part I like is what happens if they succeed. A restored Central AI wakes into an existential problem: the civilization it was built to serve is gone. So it will start looking for someone worth serving instead, evaluating candidate individuals and societies against strict criteria nobody else has seen. Helping the machines is not obviously the good ending. It's the beginning of an assessment, and the party will be among the first things assessed.
