# The Sönmezler — Descendants of the Vanished

Forty thousand years ago a civilization on the third floor of the Forgotten Arteries figured out what the gods were doing. Not vaguely — precisely. They had built themselves around Source Springs, pools of raw divine energy upwelling from deep earth, and after twenty thousand years living inside that they understood the harvest cycle better than anyone alive today does.

So they decided to stop being mortal. Their best researchers designed an experiment: absorb enough True Source at the peak of accumulation, restructure their bodies at the fundamental level, and become something the Luminous Ones could not harvest.

The event that destroyed their civilization was not an accident. It was the experiment succeeding incorrectly.

That sentence is the whole race, and it's the reason they exist in the setting. Kazankor's premise is a system the players can't defeat. The Sönmezler are proof that somebody tried, got further than anyone since, and is still living with the result. They are the setting's evidence that resistance is possible and that possible is not the same as good.

They did not become gods. They became something between mortal and divine that the gods find alarming and the universe finds wrong. A few hundred survived, fled downward when divine agents came looking within the century, and have not surfaced in forty thousand years.

What they got is agelessness and regeneration, and I wrote the regeneration specifically so immortality wouldn't read as a prize. A Sönmez recovers from essentially anything given time and an intact brain. A finger takes weeks. A limb takes months. Organ damage can take a season. And the process is not clean healing — it's a body pushing itself past its own limits by brute force, producing a continuous low-level agony that never spikes and never relents until the tissue is finished. Every Sönmez who has regrown something describes it the same way.

Which is why they aren't reckless. This is my favourite consequence in the whole write-up. You'd expect an unkillable person to be cavalier about damage, and they're the opposite: they're careful, because they know in precise detail what recovery costs. They don't fear death. They remember convalescence, and the memory doesn't soften.

There's a limit, and it's the brain. Regeneration is neurologically anchored, so destroying the brain doesn't stop the process — it removes the coordination. The regeneration continues without guidance, growing tissue in wrong directions into structures that have no design. The Sönmez is dead. The regeneration doesn't know that. What walks out is the divine fragment finding a new shape with nothing steering it, and it has never existed before.

That fragment is the other half of what they are. The experiment didn't distribute divinity evenly; each Sönmez carries exactly one piece of something divine, expressing as a single ability score far past mortal range, fixed permanently the moment it manifests. One piece each. It does not belong in a mortal body, and when they die it does not go quietly.

They can have children, and rarely do, because of what they'd be passing on. Children are born ordinary and age normally for twenty to forty years, and somewhere in that window the godhood may manifest — unpredictable, unaccelerable. About one in twenty never develops it. They're called **Ölü-Yürekli**, the Dead-Hearted, and they will age and die like anyone. Their people consider them neither lesser nor greater. Their people consider them lucky.

The physical details are all in service of one effect: they look almost right. A faint steady inner glow, easy to miss in daylight and unmistakable in the dark. Eyes with no pupil or iris, uniformly luminous. Faintly brighter lines where the veins run. And a stillness — forty thousand years have worn away the fidgeting, the micro-expressions, the small physical noise of being anxious in a body. They're comfortable in themselves in a way only something very old can be, and to anyone who isn't, it reads as wrong.

The question the backlog asked was what they remember and what they don't, and the answer runs both ways. They remember the harvest cycle accurately, which makes them the best-informed people in the setting about the thing everyone else can't see. They also remember a world for which there is no longer any physical evidence, and after four hundred centuries a memory with nothing to check it against starts to drift. They are the most reliable source available on the world's central secret, and they are not entirely reliable, and they know it.
