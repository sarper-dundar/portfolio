# Ulgarden — The Plane of Knowledge

Ulgarden records everything. Every event, every action, every secret, from the smallest interaction to the largest cosmic one, transcribed and filed as it happens. It is an endless archive of floating scrolls, crystalline tomes and glowing runes, and it stretches infinitely because it is never finished.

The design problem with a plane like this is obvious: an infinite archive of true things is a machine for ending campaigns. If the players can ask it anything, they will, and every mystery I've built collapses in one session.

I solved it with the price rather than with a locked door. Ulgarden's currency is memory. To connect at all, you perform the Ritual of the Offering of Memory: choose a real, meaningful personal memory and release it. It doesn't get stored, it isn't recoverable through the ritual — it fades, and knowledge flows in to fill the space where it was. The caster feels the loss and the liberation at once, and understands they've traded what they knew for what they can learn.

That's the whole plane in one transaction. Ulgarden charges in knowledge and understanding. Deeper sacrifices take skills and expertise — things you knew how to do — and then beliefs, opinions, and finally your sense of knowing anything at all. Players hesitate over this in a way they never hesitate over hit points, because deciding _which_ memory to give up means deciding what their character was.

There's a second ritual I like more, the Silence of the Mind: four hours under a vow of not speaking, not judging, and not forming opinions. The point isn't endurance. Ulgarden is neutral and demands neutrality from anyone reaching for it, so the caster has to become an empty vessel and stop filtering reality through what they think about it. Practitioners describe seeing things as they actually are, which is not always a kindness.

Because memory is the currency, I gave the plane a mechanic that treats it like one. A practitioner can **store** a memory rather than spend it, in an artifact or in a vault within Ulgarden itself, using an object of personal significance as the conduit. Stored memories are inaccessible to you — and untouchable by psychic, charm, or mind-control effects, which makes a vault the only genuinely secure place in the setting to put something you cannot afford to have read. The cost while it's stored is a point of Charisma or Intelligence, because you're walking around with a hole in yourself.

They can also be **exchanged**. Two willing people can trade stored memories and each gain access to the other's experience, skills, or perspective for about a day. That's the most interesting social mechanic in the Outsource system, and it has generated more trouble at my table than any damage spell, because a memory exchange is not reversible if the other party declines to give yours back.

The inhabitants sort into three groups and none of them will help you unprompted. The **Recorders** are beings of pure knowledge with quills of light, transcribing without personal desire. The **Archivists** keep the categorization and are highly attuned to distortions in time, responding immediately to anomalies. The **Seekers** are the sad ones: beings from other planes who came for the promise of understanding everything and can now never leave. They project themselves outward to gather information, but they don't go home.

The owner is **Ishkar, the All-Knowing Chronicler** — which in Kazankor means he doesn't govern Ulgarden so much as constitute it; a Plane Owner is their plane wearing a face. His eyes are said to reflect past, present and future at once. He perceives every event across time and space and interferes with none of it. He cares deeply about preserving the truth and is entirely indifferent to what the truth does to anyone. If Ishkar is distracted, the archives get disorganized and things become hard to find — which is a nicely mundane failure mode for an omniscient being.

For access, a practitioner who's been loyal can reach an Archivist directly and ask a question once per long rest. The answer comes back cryptic and incomplete, subject to interpretation. That's not me being stingy. It's the honest output of an archive that records everything and explains nothing, and it keeps Ulgarden a lead generator rather than an answer machine.

The tradeoff is that this plane rewards a specific kind of player and bores everyone else. Someone who enjoys assembling partial information will find Ulgarden the most powerful thing in the setting. Someone who wants a straight answer will find it maddening, and they aren't wrong to.
