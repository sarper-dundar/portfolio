# Prestige Classes in Kazankor

Kazankor has no class gatekeeping. Anyone can learn any system, and the barriers are access and cost rather than what it says at the top of your sheet. That creates an obvious problem for prestige classes: if nothing is locked, what exactly is a prestige class _for_?

The answer I landed on is that a prestige class isn't a permission. It's a recognition. It marks the point where a character has done something specific for long enough that the world has a name for what they've become.

That reframing changed what I build. A prestige class in my setting isn't a power upgrade with prerequisites bolted on — it's a description of a person that happens to have mechanics. The prerequisites aren't gates; they're the evidence that the description is already true.

Look at what that produces. My first batch of prestige classes has almost no magic in it at all. A Harbor Chain Marshal. An Oathsteel Drillmaster. A Quartermaster of the Long Road. A Field Chirurgeon. An Iron Mat Grappler. A Crowd Whisperer. A Shieldline Bastion. Stack & Clear Operators, which is a two-person room-clearing discipline. None of those channel anything. They're people who have done one job so many times that the job changed them, and in a setting where magic is learnable by anyone, the genuinely distinctive characters are often the ones who got very good at something ordinary.

The second batch does the opposite thing, and this is where the setting's structure pays off. Because the magic systems are separate rather than subdivisions of one system, the interesting space is _between_ them. A Glyph-Breaker is built specifically to unmake Tamga. A Margin Walker specialises in defending against Outsource. A Reverse Summoner works the obligation side of a planar contract from the wrong end. A Contract Surgeon operates on agreements. A Living Scroll Host carries inscription in their own body. A Chain-Reaction Scriptor builds Tamga arrays that cascade. A Dissonance Assassin uses Songs to kill. An Echo-Field Archivist works in residue.

Every one of those exists because two systems touch and nobody had formalised what happens at the seam. That's the most productive place to look for a prestige class in a setting like this — not "what if a Tamga user got stronger," but "what does a person who has spent fifteen years specifically countering Tamga users know that nobody wrote down?"

The third group is the one I'm most attached to, and it's the most Kazankor-specific. Prestiges named in Turkish for things only this world produces. Hasat Simsarı, the harvest broker. Silinmiş İsim, the erased name. Savaş Borcu, war debt. Son Adım, the last step. Spor Paktı, a formal accommodation with the Marnok. Zehir Haritacısı, the poison cartographer. Kan Damgası, blood-brand. Those aren't builds. They're roles the setting generated on its own, and I only noticed they existed because something in play kept pointing at a shape with no name attached to it.

There are two rules I hold to across all of them.

The first is that a prestige class layers rather than replaces. The Rüzgar continues advancing as a Sülde bender; the prestige sits on top of the existing practice. If taking a prestige means abandoning the thing that earned it, the prestige is a different character rather than a deeper one.

The second is that at least one prerequisite has to be a scene rather than a number. Category levels and proficiencies are easy to check and easy to farm. What actually makes a prestige feel earned is a requirement like the Rüzgar's: a moment, agreed with the DM, where the character chose the wind over something safer or stronger, and the wind chose to stay. You cannot grind that. You either have that moment in your history or you don't.

The cost of designing this way is quantity. I have far more prestige classes than any campaign will use, because I keep writing them when play reveals one. That's a lot of documents for content most tables will never open — the same problem the Alchemist's fifty-one paths have. I've decided the ratio is acceptable, because the value isn't in the count. It's that when a player describes what their character has become, there's a reasonable chance the world already has a word for it.
