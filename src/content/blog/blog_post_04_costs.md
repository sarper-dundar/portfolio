# Designing Costs That Actually Cost

Every power system in Kazankor charges for use. That's easy to say and hard to build, because most costs in games aren't really costs — they're accounting steps. A number goes down, you write a smaller number, nothing about the character changes. I wanted the four main systems to charge in ways a player would feel, and I ended up with four different shapes of cost rather than one shared meter. That was deliberate, and it's the closest thing I have to a design principle.

**The Alchemist charges for inconsistency.** Every formula carries a Digestion Condition: the change it makes has to be lived, or it doesn't settle. An Alchemist who behaves in direct contradiction to what a formula asks accumulates Corruption — cosmetic at first, then a body that starts making permanent changes without being asked, and at the far end Dissolution, where the body continues and the person using it does not. The cost isn't paid in resources at all. It's paid in behavior.

**Songs of Creation charge on two tracks, then charge again per Song.** Holding a Song means a Constitution and a Wisdom save every turn against a DC that climbs by one each time. Failures accumulate as Strain and Fracture, and the two aren't interchangeable — Strain takes your legs, Fracture takes your ability to hold a pattern at all. On top of that, every Song has a Fault belonging to it specifically. Songs that protect take something from the singer's own safety; songs that harm leave residue in their perception. Faults aren't punishments for a failed roll. They're the price of the particular thing being asked, paid whether it goes well or not.

**Outsource charges whatever the counterparty wants.** Six sacrifice tiers, Trivial up to Severe. The tier tells you how much it hurts but not what gets taken — that's the plane's business. Ulgarden wants knowledge, so you pay in things you knew how to do. Periland wants joy and good memory. Tünkurkan wants free will. Kazırgan wants blood and moral boundary. Same tier, four different wounds — and a practitioner holding several connections is servicing several unrelated debts at once.

**Ruh Usta charges by the shape of the pool itself.** Soul power isn't a tank you drain; it's bidirectional, with an optimal zone it pressures itself back toward. Above the ceiling you get up to +3, but the surplus bleeds off at roughly 7% per turn, so strength is a window rather than a reserve. Below the floor, abilities cost 10%, then 25%, then 50% more — being low makes getting lower cheaper, which is a spiral rather than a wall.

The rule underneath all four is that a cost has to land on something the player already cares about: how their character behaves, what they remember, who they owe, what they'll be capable of next month. A cost that only touches a number gets optimized around within two sessions.

The tradeoff is bookkeeping, and it isn't small. The subtler failure is worse — a cost that bites too hard produces a player who quietly never uses the ability, and an unused system is a worse outcome than a cheap one. That's why every track has a documented way down. The costs are meant to be pressure the player manages, not a ratchet that only turns one way.
