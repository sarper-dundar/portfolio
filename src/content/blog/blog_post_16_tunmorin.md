# Tünmorin — The Plane of Soul Recycling

Tünmorin is where the dead go, and it is not an afterlife. It's a process.

Souls arrive and are guided into forgetting. They travel across plains, through forests of light, along rivers of memory, and as they go they release everything — the life they had, the people in it, who they were. What comes out the other end is a newborn spirit, pure and formless, and it gets sent back out into the planes to live again. No soul is lost. No soul is preserved either.

Deciding on that shape settled several other questions at once. There's no heaven or hell in Kazankor to reward or punish anyone, because the machinery isn't moral. Tünmorin doesn't judge a soul, it _empties_ one, and the emptying is the point. The universe would stagnate if the dead accumulated, so the cycle keeps moving and the cost is that nothing carries forward.

Which makes necromancy in this setting a very specific kind of crime. It isn't offensive to the gods and it isn't unclean. It's an interruption in a supply chain. A soul held back from Tünmorin hasn't been cleansed and hasn't been reborn, and the plane notices the gap. Raising the dead here means taking something out of the cycle and keeping it, and the beings whose job is the cycle will eventually come looking for it.

The staff reflect that. The **Shepherds of Souls** are calm and serene and guide new arrivals through their transition without distress — genuinely kind, and completely committed to the outcome. The **Watchers** handle enforcement: they exist to ensure no soul deviates or manages to hold on to its memories, and they're described as stern but fair, which is the most bureaucratic phrasing in my cosmology and entirely intentional.

The owner is **Oruntal, the Soul Weaver** — and as with every Plane Owner in Kazankor, he doesn't administer Tünmorin, he _is_ it, expressed as a being. He appears as shifting mist and light, holding threads of memory that he weaves as souls pass through. He's compassionate and patient, almost parental — and completely unwavering. If a soul resists forgetting, Oruntal severs its ties by force, carefully, because the process can harm a soul that fights it. He never lets sentiment interfere with the work. His limitation is the part I care about: he cannot influence where a soul goes next or what its life will be. He only prepares it. Every soul that leaves him is genuinely unwritten.

If Oruntal is weakened or distracted, souls linger and some cling to memory. That backs up into the other planes, and it's where most of my hauntings come from — not malice, just a queue that stopped moving.

What a practitioner draws from Tünmorin follows the same logic. The price is memory and identity: personal history, fragments of self, attachments, the sense of being a continuous person. You pay in exactly what the plane strips from the dead, which is the neatest correspondence I managed anywhere in Outsource.

It gave me two mechanics. **Echoed Memories** means casting repeatedly in one place leaves residue — faint traces of the caster's memories and emotional state that later visitors pick up as flashes of insight or unexplained feeling. With deliberate effort a practitioner can amplify this into a message, paying by sacrificing their own ability to feel a particular emotion for a day, or their attachment to the event they're recording. It's a way to write on a location instead of on a page, and it means places in my world can be haunted by someone who is still alive.

**Memory Bonding** links a practitioner to another creature so they share thoughts and feelings, temporarily or permanently. The permanent version costs a fragment of identity — a minor personality trait, gone for as long as the bond holds — and creates a vulnerability running both ways. Two characters permanently bonded through Tünmorin are stronger together and each slightly less themselves, which is a fair description of the thing it's modelling.

The honest weakness is that Tünmorin is thinner on encounter material than the other planes. It's a well-defined process with good staff and a clear owner, and I've never had a reason to build a fight in it. That may be correct — a place whose whole function is gentle, mandatory forgetting probably shouldn't be a dungeon — but it does mean the plane shows up in my campaigns as a consequence rather than a destination.
