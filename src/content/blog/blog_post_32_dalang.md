# The Dalang

Most campaign organizations are things the players join. The Dalang is one they built, and running it has taught me more about faction design than any faction I wrote for them to discover.

The name is Mongolian — cunning, the hidden hand behind the puppet. It's used only inside the organization, as a single-word identifier between members, and if anyone outside hears it in that context it's treated as a serious breach. Nothing else in the campaign knows it exists. Not the guild, not the spy network, not the people the players consider friends.

It has three branches for three functions. **Kırıcı** — the breakers — damage systems, not people: operatives who go somewhere and leave it less functional than they found it, through broken infrastructure, corrupted supply chains, disabled mechanisms. **İplik** — the thread — gathers information through sustained infiltration and long-term covers, people who become someone else and stay that way for months. **Kalkan** — the shield — is counter-intelligence and asset security, the ones who watch the watchers.

The most important line in the whole document is what it isn't. The Dalang is not an assassination service and doesn't kill as a primary tool. Recruits drawn to killing for its own sake are screened out during selection.

That constraint is doing an enormous amount of work, and not for moral reasons. The practical argument is the one I actually wrote down: killing generates investigations, property damage generates insurance claims. A dead official is a manhunt with the empire's full attention. A foundry that keeps failing inspection is a procurement dispute. One of those ends your organization and the other one is Tuesday.

It also solves the problem that usually kills player-run factions at the table, which is that an organization capable of killing anyone quickly becomes the answer to every problem, and then the campaign is about logistics. An organization that can only break things has to be _aimed_, and aiming it requires the players to work out what actually holds a target together. That's a much better use of a session.

The cover architecture runs in three layers and my note says plainly that without all three it collapses within a year. The physical layer is two sealed rooms in the colosseum undercroft that aren't on any floor plan, reached through the beast-handler area so there's legitimate foot traffic to hide inside, with a second site three hours out in the foothills registered in the players' own names as an old mining survey station. The other layers cover the money and the people, because a secret organization is discovered through its accounts and its recruits long before anyone finds its rooms.

Recruitment was the decision I second-guessed most and would make again. Every member is a fresh recruit with no prior institutional loyalties. That's slower — you can't buy an experienced spy, you have to make one — and it means the organization is young and its people are inexperienced for the first stretch of its existence. What it buys is that nobody in it came from somewhere else, which means nobody in it is still reporting to somewhere else. Given that the campaign's other intelligence operations are full of people who were turned years ago and don't remember being turned, that's worth the slowness.

The philosophy line I wrote for it is short: professional, precise, directed. The players decide what the organization does; the organization executes. That's a deliberate refusal to give it a personality of its own. I've seen player factions become NPCs — a faction with opinions starts arguing, and then the players are negotiating with their own asset. The Dalang has competence and no agenda. If it does something objectionable, one of the players decided it should.

What it gives them mechanically is invisible reach into situations they cannot touch directly. A party that's too recognisable to walk into a building now has someone who was hired there four months ago. That solves a specific late-campaign problem, where the characters have become famous enough that their greatest strength — being extremely capable and well known — is the thing stopping them from doing anything quietly.

The honest cost is that it's a second game. Running the Dalang properly means tracking operatives who aren't at the table, missions resolving between sessions, and covers that decay if they aren't maintained. I keep its file physically separate from every other document for the campaign, which tells you how much of a liability a leak would be — not in the fiction, but at my own table.
