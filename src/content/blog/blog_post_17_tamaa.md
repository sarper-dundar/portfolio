# Tamaa — The Void

Kazırgan destroys things carefully. Tamaa destroys because it cannot do anything else.

The distinction matters more than it sounds. Kazırgan's beings calculate what to end and why; they see themselves as necessary. Tamaa's don't calculate anything. The plane is unrelenting entropy — mountains collapse, ground disintegrates underfoot, nothing stays whole for long — and the air itself carries a chaotic pressure that makes even composed beings struggle not to start tearing at whatever is nearby.

Its population is three groups. Most are **abominations and foul creatures**, mindless, driven by instinct to consume. Above them sit the **fallen angels** — once servants of the gods, now intelligent, cunning, and comfortable in the role, directing the mindless hordes toward destruction that actually accomplishes something. And then the **accidental inhabitants**: beings who dabbled in dark power or reached for destruction without understanding it, and were twisted by the plane into monstrous forms driven by the same instinct as everything else. That third group is the one I use most, because it means anyone can end up there.

The backlog I was working from described Tamaa's demons as unnamed, and that's largely still true and largely deliberate. Naming a devil makes it negotiable. Tamaa's things mostly aren't. The fallen angels are the exception — the ones who can be talked to — and the fact that the only conversational beings in the void are the ones who chose it is the point.

The owner is **Skerith, the Ruinous Hunger** — and here I should be precise about a thing I took too long to settle. A Plane Owner is not a god who rules a plane. They _are_ the plane, expressed as a being. Skerith is Tamaa with a face on; the two names describe one entity. That's why the plane's condition tracks his state rather than obeying his orders. When he rages, Tamaa becomes a maelstrom and its creatures frenzy. When he's weakened or distracted, the destruction slows to a creep. Nobody is issuing commands. The place is simply having the mood.

He's also the only Plane Owner in my cosmology with almost no intelligence. Monstrous, ever-changing, running on primal instinct, and carrying one specific undying hatred: the True Source itself. Not the gods, not mortals, not creation as an idea. The substrate. He wants the bottom of the universe gone.

Which points at the thing that makes Tamaa structurally dangerous rather than just violent. The beings of this plane hate the True Source so much that they occasionally manage to touch its **Opposite** — and using both at once produces something close to omnipotent, unstable enough to threaten reality outright. It's rare and it's forbidden, and both sides of creation treat anyone who attempts it as an abomination to be hunted. That single loophole is the largest existential threat in the setting, and it exists because the void's hatred is more focused than anyone else's ambition.

For a practitioner, Tamaa offers stillness, siphoned vitality and accelerated decay — fields where energy stagnates and spells stall, auras that age everything nearby, effects that punch holes in reality. The price is a **Void Corruption** track running one to ten, and its shape is different from the Alchemist's. Corruption here isn't the body making decisions without permission. It's the body ceasing.

It starts as occasional numbness and lethargy, a feeling of being slightly disconnected. By the middle it's genuine erosion — physical fatigue, difficulty feeling emotions, memory lapses, then apathy and mild hallucinations with measurable stat reductions. At six, parts of the body become faintly transparent, and enemies can sense the corruption and press it. By eight the caster is physically dissipating in places. At ten they are gone: no form, no consciousness, dissolved into the void.

Two details make it bite. Past level five, corruption is permanent without powerful intervention — the track stops being something you manage and becomes something you've become. And at the higher levels, casting a Tamaa spell risks void shock, so the ability that got you here is the thing accelerating the ending. Every other cost system in Kazankor eventually lets you climb back. This one has a point of no return written into it, which is what a void plane ought to have.

Contact is correspondingly grim. The Ritual of Void Communion is four hours in complete darkness and complete silence with a memory sacrificed as payment, during which the caster feels themselves fading and becoming less real, and hears what my notes call the call of nothingness. It's a high check and a genuinely unpleasant scene to run, and I think it should be. Nobody should connect to Tamaa by accident or in a hurry.

The design tension I haven't fully solved is that Tamaa is more useful to me as an antagonist than as an option. A player who takes this connection is on a timer they can only slow, and the interesting version of that story is short. I've come to treat it as a deliberate late-campaign choice rather than a build — something a character reaches for when they've decided the cost is acceptable because of what they intend to do with the time.
