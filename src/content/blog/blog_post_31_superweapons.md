# Kharos-Vaul's Superweapons — Requiem Engine and Soulfire Mirror

The problem with a superweapon in a campaign is that it's usually either a countdown the party stops in one scene or a plot device that can never actually fire. I wanted two that were real threats, buildable by mortals, and beatable — but beatable through understanding rather than damage.

The **Soulfire Mirror** is the one people know about. A thirty-foot obsidian mirror two hundred feet beneath the High Marshal's war room, capable of erasing any city on the continent. It's whispered among high officers and feared abroad, and in Kazankor terms it's far more transgressive than a large scrying-cannon: it channels raw True Source without a living channeler. Mortals built it who had no right to attempt it and it worked.

The reason it works is the part I'm proudest of. No mortal can grasp, hold and release raw True Source at city-killing scale and survive — the grasp fails, the hold escalates past any endurance, the release destroys the channeler. So three hundred years ago the founding Spymaster and Arch-Artificer distributed the channeling cycle across a hundred souls at once. One hundred imperial soldiers who volunteered. Their skeletons line the vault's niches; their souls stay threaded through the Mirror's Tamga lattice, each carrying one percent of a burden no single person could hold.

Willingness isn't ceremony there — it's mechanism. An unwilling soul fails the grasp, and one failure in the lattice collapses the whole channel.

Which hands the players the best counterplay in the room. Souls that genuinely _revoke_ consent weaken the lattice, and past the tenth withdrawal every firing carries cumulative failure risk. A party that reaches the martyrs — through a Ruh Usta perceiving them directly, through dream contact with the threads, through someone who knows the release procedure — can beat a continental weapon by talking to it. Some of the hundred have been holding for a very long time. Some of them have changed their minds.

The rest of the design follows from the same instinct. Firing takes an hour of charge that cannot be shortened, and the famous hum is a hundred souls straining in unison, audible through the citadel, so using it puts the whole garrison on alert and makes escape nearly impossible. Targeting doesn't scry — the array reads a living officer's memory of a place they personally visited, which means no ward at the target helps and nothing can be detected there, but also that the Mirror can only strike places a keyed officer has actually been. Deny the memory and you deny the shot. And it can't fire without a living general of sufficient rank present to speak the consent-phrase, so removing one person removes the weapon.

Three hundred years, one firing — a rebellious province annihilated and struck from every record. Two consequences hang off that. The vault walls carry three centuries of Tamutash accretion plus one catastrophic discharge, making them the single richest deposit in the empire, unharvested because almost nobody knows the room exists. And raw True Source at that scale is a flare visible to the Luminous Ones. That firing was noticed. Something has been paying closer attention to Kharos-Vaul ever since, patiently, on a divine timescale.

The **Requiem Engine** is the other one, and nobody is supposed to know it exists. Beneath the Phylactery Forge sits a soul-machine built from the compressed souls of every creature that has died inside the city's walls for two hundred years. In cosmological terms it's a standing atrocity against Tünmorin — a deliberate hole where the plane's collection should happen, intercepting every death in a three-mile radius before the cycle of renewal can receive it.

It was built by the previous Spymaster, whose name has been struck from the records, who believed the empire could only survive by harnessing death and made a pact to learn how. The entity he bargained with taught him to make the hole and takes its cut of what falls through. When his body failed he bound his own consciousness into the Engine as its controlling intelligence, and he is still in there.

Only three know about it: the Arch-Artificer, the current Spymaster, and the Engine itself. Not the High Marshal. The Arch-Artificer found it eighty years ago, recognised it immediately as an industrialised version of his own forbidden research, and chose to maintain rather than destroy it on the judgement that it was too dangerous to leave unmanaged. The Spymaster knows it through her predecessor's records, and visits him once a year.

What makes these two work as a pair is that they're opposites in every way that matters. The Mirror is known, deterrent, and public enough to shape foreign policy. The Engine is secret, passive, and has been quietly harvesting the city's dead for two centuries while everyone above it went about their business. One is the empire's threat. The other is its debt.

And neither is beaten with a sword. The Mirror falls to consent withdrawn, a memory denied, or an officer removed. The Engine is a moral problem with a person inside it who has had two hundred years to think about what he did. That's the standard I now hold myself to for anything at this scale: if the answer is "hit it until it stops," I haven't finished designing it.
