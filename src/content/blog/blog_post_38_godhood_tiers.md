# Tiers of Godhood in Kazankor

The mistake I made early was building gods on one scale. More powerful, less powerful, all the same kind of thing. That works until a player asks a reasonable question — who made the planes, who enforces the rules the gods follow — and the only available answers are "the strongest god" or a shrug.

So Kazankor's divine hierarchy has five layers, and the thing I care about most is that mortals know about exactly two of them.

Start with the beginning. Before existence there was only endless primordial water — the True Source — and above it flew **Ertene**, an eternal white goose, circling forever. From the depths of that water emerged two beings: **Oryana**, the White Mother, who is light and motion and change and the flow of the True Source; and **Nogana**, the Black Mother, who is darkness and stillness and the flow of the Opposite. Neither is good and neither is evil. They are keepers of balance, and the universe they made is zero-sum by design — every creation balanced somewhere by a return.

Those three are Layer 1 — the **Üçler**, the Three. No mortal knows they exist. They aren't worshipped, because there is nobody to do the worshipping.

**Layer 1.5 is the Plane Owners**, and the reason for the odd number is that they don't sit cleanly in the ladder. They're ancient beings just below the Üçler, and the crucial fact about them is one I only settled recently: a Plane Owner _is their plane, expressed as a being_. Skerith is not a god who rules Tamaa. Skerith is Tamaa, with a face on. Ishkar is Ulgarden. Ragûl is Kazırgan. Writing "a rival to Tamaa" in a god's file is correct, because the plane and the being are the same entity described two ways.

That resolution fixed something that had been quietly bothering me for a long time — why the state of a plane tracks the mood of its owner. Periland grows wild when Lirael is delighted and turns erratic when she's weakened. Kazırgan's destruction is measured while Ragûl is focused and becomes dangerous when he isn't. I had been writing that as a god's influence over territory. It's simpler than that. The plane is having the mood.

**Layer 2 is the Atalar** — the primordials proper, and the Pantheon of Synir is the one I've actually finished. Synir and five children, born from the Shattering of the Star Forges. There are more pantheons at this tier and I haven't written them yet, which is a gap I'm comfortable leaving open, because a hierarchy the players can see the whole of isn't doing its job.

Mortals don't know the Atalar exist either. Where folklore brushes against them it calls them the **Unseen Lords**, and the scholars who suspect something is up there call them the **Veiled Pantheon**. All of those names point at the same beings, and the stories that mention them are more accurate than anyone telling them realises.

A note on vocabulary, because I've tripped over it myself. **Üçler** and **Atalar** are the Turkish names for Layers 1 and 2, and they describe exactly the same hierarchy as the numbered version — only the wording differs. The word that causes trouble is _primordial_. Loosely it gets attached to the Üçler, since they're the oldest things there are. Precisely, the primordials are the Atalar: Synir's pantheon and the others at that tier. The Three aren't primordial beings. They're what the primordials came out of.

**Layer 3 is the Luminous Ones** — around sixty gods across fourteen sub-pantheons, and these are the ones mortals actually mean when they say gods. They are visible. They send light. They have names, portfolios, rivalries, temples, and politics. They can be prayed to, contracted with, and argued about. They also run the harvest, which means the beings the world knows best are middle management.

**Layer 4 is the Illumined Monarchs** — mortals who ascended, which takes level 30 and a god seed and does not take a particular class. Mortals know they exist, which makes this the only other layer the world is aware of. That's the whole visible cosmology from below: the Luminous Ones above, and the handful of people who climbed.

The decision I'd defend hardest is that the power scale between layers is deliberately vague, and I'm not going to fix it. There are no numbers comparing a Plane Owner to a member of Synir's pantheon, and there won't be. Mortals cannot perceive the difference, so the difference has no gameplay function, and putting a figure on it would only invite the exact conversation the setting is built to avoid — the one where players work out what they'd need to beat a god.

What the structure actually buys me is dramatic irony with a specific shape. The gods the players can meet, bargain with, and occasionally hurt are three layers from the top. Above them sit beings born from an event no mortal has heard of, above those sit the planes themselves walking around with opinions, and above everything are the Three, who are the water this is all floating in. And the Luminous Ones — the ones running the farm, engineering the wars, collecting the yield — don't know about most of that either.

Which makes the harvest look different depending on where you stand. From inside it, it's the largest fact about the world. From Layer 2, it's a regional operation by beings who don't know they're being watched. From Layer 1, it may not have started yet.
