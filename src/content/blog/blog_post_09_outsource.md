# Outsource — Borrowing From Other Planes

The core principle of Outsource is one line I wrote at the top of the document and then designed backwards from: you do not own this power, you borrow it, and the plane can take it back at any time.

Every other system in Kazankor gives the practitioner something. Tamga gives you a skill. A formula gives you a changed body. A Song gives you a pattern you now carry. Outsource gives you a relationship, and relationships are revocable. That's the whole appeal and the whole problem.

The system runs on five pillars, which is a slightly grand way of saying there are five things you have to keep doing. You establish a connection. You cast with it. You pay the price. You accept the risk. You negotiate bargains if you want more than the standard rate.

Establishing a connection is the part that surprises players. It isn't a downtime activity and I've been firm about that — you cannot schedule a meeting with a Fey Lord. Each plane has its own ritual with its own components and its own Arcana or Religion check, and passing the check isn't enough on its own. There's an initial sacrifice, and then there's a step I care about more than the roll: you have to actually accept the connection. If you resist the plane's influence while asking for its power, the connection comes out weak or fails outright. You can't do this at arm's length.

A practitioner can hold more than one connection, and that's where the system gets its shape. Every plane you're connected to is a separate relationship with a separate price and separate expectations, running at the same time. Two connections mean two ongoing debts in unrelated currencies. Severing one isn't clean either — it needs its own ritual and its own cost, and even afterwards you may keep physical changes, strange dreams, or a stretch of diminished capacity.

Holding several also produces **synergies**, which was the reward I wanted for the risk of spreading yourself. Some pairs amplify each other. Some interfere badly enough to create complications neither plane would have caused alone. A third connection opens combinations most practitioners never live to document. So the strategic question isn't which plane is strongest — it's which planes can stand being in the same person.

The result is that an Outsource practitioner isn't collecting spells, they're managing a portfolio of obligations. A player who has been drawing on Ulgarden for a year and takes on Kazırgan alongside it is a different character afterwards, and everyone at the table watched it happen.

Sacrifice is where the per-plane texture lives. There are six tiers, from Trivial through Minor, Light, Moderate, Heavy, and Severe. The tier tells you how much it hurts and how recoverable it is — Trivial heals immediately, Light needs a long rest, Moderate needs a ritual, Heavy is permanent short of powerful magic, Severe needs divine intervention. What the tier does not tell you is _what gets taken_. That's the plane's business.

Tözyar takes temporal perception, spatial awareness, your sense of where and when you are. Ulgarden takes knowledge — things you knew how to do, and then your beliefs and your sense of knowing. Uştmay takes personal freedom, choice, individuality, creativity. Kazırgan takes blood, vitality, and moral boundary. Periland takes joy, good memory, and wonder. Tünkurkan takes free will. Tünmorin takes identity fragments and personal history. Tamaa takes existence itself.

So two practitioners paying a Moderate sacrifice for equally powerful effects can end up damaged in completely unrelated ways. One has lost a skill. One has lost a year of feeling anything pleasant. I find this much more interesting than a shared hit point cost, and it means the choice of plane is a choice about what kind of person you're willing to become.

Bargains sit on top of the standard rate. If the tiered sacrifices aren't enough, you can contact an entity directly and negotiate — offer service, offer something it wants, haggle over terms. The document's warning to itself is the honest one: entities are clever and will try to trick you. A sealed bargain is binding, and failing to honour it costs you the power and invites whatever the entity considers appropriate.

The cost of building it this way is that Outsource asks more of the DM than any other system I've made. Every sacrifice is adjudicated rather than subtracted, and a DM who lets a player pay in something that doesn't actually cost them has quietly turned the most dangerous system in the setting into the safest one. The tier tables tell you the weight. They can't tell you whether this particular memory mattered to this particular character, and that's the part that has to be decided at the table, every time.
